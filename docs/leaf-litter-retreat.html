Overview

Past studies, our own observations and many community-science reports on iNaturalist (Fig. S1 in the manuscript) suggest that green anoles retreat into the leaf litter during cold weather. Below we test whether cold temperatures trigger this behavioral shift and quantify how much the leaf litter buffers green anoles from cold. To do this, we combine standardized night surveys in Atlanta, GA, with temperatures recorded by operative temperature models (OTMs) deployed in the canopy and the leaf litter at five locations across the species’ range.

Each OTM consisted of an iButton suspended within a 2 x 7 cm painted copper pipe, secured with corks. OTMs with these characteristics match the thermal properties of similarly sized Anolis lizards and thus closely approximate the body temperatures lizards would experience in a given microhabitat. We affixed OTMs to branches at ~2 m height (i.e., “canopy”) or buried them 5-10 cm deep in the leaf litter, and programmed them to record temperature hourly between September 2025 and May 2026. Before analysis, we excluded leaf-litter OTMs that became exposed during deployment (i.e., when wind or animals displaced the litter covering them). Because the litter dampens daily temperature fluctuations, we identified these OTMs as those whose average daily temperature range in January exceeded 50% of that of canopy OTMs at the same location. This criterion flagged two leaf-litter OTMs in Atlanta, whose daily temperature range increased progressively over the deployment, consistent with the gradual loss of their litter cover. The processed data are stored in data/otms-data.rds.

Green anoles retreat to the leaf litter

We conducted 34 standardized night surveys between September 2025 and April 2026 in a semi-natural area on the Georgia Institute of Technology campus in Atlanta, GA (33.75°N, -84.4°W), where green anoles are locally abundant. The site has small trees and bushes, interrupted by natural and artificial paths, and offers no other microhabitats for lizards to retreat to (i.e., no rock crevices or cavities in large trees) besides the leaf litter. In each survey, teams of 2-13 researchers walked a standardized 720 m transect for 1 hour, starting 1 hour after sunset, scanning vegetation for lizards and recording the time of each observation.

Below we summarise the survey data into the presence and number of green anoles observed per survey, and the operative temperatures recorded by the OTMs in the canopy and leaf litter during the survey period (i.e., between 1 and 2 hours after sunset, which we calculate with the suncalc package). We then plot how the probability of observing lizards (A) and the number of lizards observed (B) changed through time alongside canopy and leaf litter temperatures (Fig. S6 in the manuscript). Throughout this section, CTmin refers to the lowest population-average CTmin we measured across the species’ range (6.8 °C, in Knoxville, TN; see Green anoles live beyond their physiological cold tolerance). We use this value because it represents the cold tolerance of the most cold-tolerant population, so any temperatures below it are below the physiological limit of every population we measured, including the one in Atlanta.

Next, we determine the temperature that triggers green anoles to retreat into the leaf litter. We focus on the presence of lizards rather than their abundance to account for the variation in the number of observers between surveys. Because our surveys span fall, winter and spring, canopy temperatures are partly confounded with season, and lizards could respond to seasonal cues such as photoperiod rather than to temperature itself. To disentangle these effects, we model the presence of lizards as a function of canopy operative temperature, photoperiod and their interaction, which allows the effect of temperature to differ between short and long days. Photoperiod declines from September to the winter solstice and increases again towards April, so surveys with similar photoperiods occurred both in fall and in spring, under a wide range of temperatures. We fit a generalized linear model with the formula:

\[ P_i \sim Bernoulli(p_i) \]

\[ logit(p_i) = \alpha + \beta_T \cdot (T_i - \bar{T}) + \beta_D \cdot (D_i - \bar{D}) + \beta_{T \cdot D} \cdot (T_i - \bar{T}) \cdot (D_i - \bar{D}) \]

where the observed presence of lizards in survey \(i\) (\(P_i\)) follows a Bernoulli distribution with an observation probability (\(p_i\)) that depends on the average operative temperature recorded across all canopy OTMs during the survey period, between 1 and 2 hours after sunset (\(T_i\)), and on photoperiod (\(D_i\); hours between sunrise and sunset in Atlanta on the day of the survey, calculated with the suncalc package). We center temperature and photoperiod on their means across surveys (\(\bar{T}\) and \(\bar{D}\)), so that \(\beta_T\) describes the effect of temperature at an average photoperiod, \(\beta_D\) the effect of photoperiod at an average temperature, and \(\beta_{T \cdot D}\) how the effect of temperature changes with photoperiod. We test the contribution of each predictor with likelihood-ratio tests (LRT): for the interaction, we compare the full model with a model without the interaction; for temperature and photoperiod, we compare the full model with a model without that predictor and its interaction (i.e., testing whether the predictor has any effect, either on its own or through the interaction). The results of this model are presented in Table S5 of the manuscript.

From the fitted model, we then invert the logit function to obtain the temperature at which the observation probability equals a series of values of interest, at the average photoperiod:

\[ T = \bar{T} + \frac{logit(p) - \alpha}{\beta_T} \]

We obtain 95% confidence intervals for these temperatures with the delta method, using the variance-covariance matrix of \(\alpha\) and \(\beta_T\).

Model observation probability as a function of canopy temperature and photoperiod 
# calculate photoperiod (hours between sunrise and sunset) on each survey date
survey_photoperiod <- getSunlightTimes(
  date = survey_sum$date,
  lat = my_lat,
  lon = my_lon,
  keep = c("sunrise", "sunset"),
  tz = "America/New_York"
) |>
  transmute(
    date,
    photoperiod = as.numeric(difftime(sunset, sunrise, units = "hours"))
  )

# combine survey data with canopy temperatures and photoperiod
survey_sum_model <- survey_sum |>
  left_join(
    filter(otm_data_ATL, microhabitat == "branch"),
    by = c("year", "doy", "date")
  ) |>
  left_join(survey_photoperiod, by = "date") |>
  mutate(
    temp_c = mean_temp - mean(mean_temp),
    photoperiod_c = photoperiod - mean(photoperiod)
  )
mean_photoperiod <- mean(survey_sum_model$photoperiod)
mean_survey_temp <- mean(survey_sum_model$mean_temp)

# main model: canopy temperature, photoperiod and their interaction
mod <- glm(
  presence ~ temp_c * photoperiod_c,
  data = survey_sum_model,
  family = binomial(link = "logit")
)

# likelihood-ratio tests: the interaction (1 df), and any effect of
# temperature or photoperiod, either on its own or through the interaction
# (2 df)
lrt_vs <- function(reduced) {
  a <- anova(
    glm(reduced, data = survey_sum_model, family = binomial(link = "logit")),
    mod,
    test = "LRT"
  )
  tibble(lrt = a$Deviance[2], df = a$Df[2], p = a$`Pr(>Chi)`[2])
}
mod_lrt <- bind_rows(
  temp_c = lrt_vs(presence ~ photoperiod_c),
  photoperiod_c = lrt_vs(presence ~ temp_c),
  `temp_c:photoperiod_c` = lrt_vs(presence ~ temp_c + photoperiod_c),
  .id = "term"
)
lrt_row <- function(term) mod_lrt[mod_lrt$term == term, ]

# get temperature thresholds (and delta-method 95% CIs) for probabilities at
# the average photoperiod
probabilities <- c(0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.25, 0.5)
mod_vcov <- vcov(mod)[1:2, 1:2]
thresholds <- tibble(probability = probabilities) |>
  mutate(
    temperature = mean_survey_temp +
      (qlogis(probability) - coef(mod)[1]) / coef(mod)[2],
    se = sqrt(purrr::map_dbl(probability, \(p) {
      grad <- c(-1, -(qlogis(p) - coef(mod)[1]) / coef(mod)[2]) / coef(mod)[2]
      as.numeric(t(grad) %*% mod_vcov %*% grad)
    })),
    lower = temperature - qnorm(0.975) * se,
    upper = temperature + qnorm(0.975) * se
  )

# define retreating temperature (Tret) as the temperature at which
# observation probability falls below 20%
tret_temp <- round(thresholds$temperature[thresholds$probability == 0.2], 2)
tret_ci <- round(
  unlist(thresholds[thresholds$probability == 0.2, c("lower", "upper")]), 2
)

# predict observation probability (and 95% CI) from the main model
predict_presence <- function(newdata) {
  pred <- predict(mod, newdata = newdata, type = "link", se.fit = TRUE)
  newdata |>
    mutate(
      fit = plogis(pred$fit),
      lower = plogis(pred$fit - qnorm(0.975) * pred$se.fit),
      upper = plogis(pred$fit + qnorm(0.975) * pred$se.fit)
    )
}
pred_temp <- predict_presence(
  tibble(mean_temp = seq(-2, 25, by = 0.1), photoperiod_c = 0) |>
    mutate(temp_c = mean_temp - mean_survey_temp)
)
pred_photoperiod <- predict_presence(
  tibble(
    photoperiod_c = seq(
      min(survey_sum_model$photoperiod_c),
      max(survey_sum_model$photoperiod_c),
      by = 0.01
    ),
    temp_c = 0
  )
) |>
  mutate(photoperiod = photoperiod_c + mean_photoperiod)

The leaf litter buffers environmental temperatures

To quantify the thermal buffering green anoles experience in the leaf litter, we compare the minimum temperatures recorded by OTMs in the canopy and in the leaf litter across the five locations where we deployed them: Miami (MIA), Port Saint Lucie (PSL), Orlando (ORL), Gainesville (GNV) and Atlanta (ATL). We focus on January, the coldest month of the year, and obtain the minimum temperature recorded by each OTM on each night.

Buffering across latitudes

We test whether minimum temperatures differ between microhabitats and across latitudes using a linear mixed model. Each OTM contributes one minimum temperature per night and all OTMs at a location experience the same weather on a given night, so these observations are not independent. We therefore include random intercepts for each OTM, for each location and for each night at each location:

\[ T_i \sim Normal(t_i, \sigma) \]

\[ t_i = \alpha + \beta_L \cdot (L_i - \bar{L}) + \beta_M \cdot M_i + \beta_{L \cdot M} \cdot (L_i - \bar{L}) \cdot M_i + a_{O[i]} + b_{S[i]} + c_{N[i]} \]

where the nightly minimum operative temperature (\(T\)) depends on latitude (\(L\)), an indicator variable (\(M\)) identifying the microhabitat (\(M = 0\) for canopy and \(M = 1\) for leaf litter), and their interaction, and \(a_O\), \(b_S\) and \(c_N\) are normally distributed random intercepts for each OTM (\(O\)), location (\(S\)) and night at each location (\(N\)), with standard deviations \(\sigma_O\), \(\sigma_S\) and \(\sigma_N\). We center latitude on the average latitude of the five locations (\(\bar{L}\)), so that \(\beta_M\) is the average buffering provided by the leaf litter at an average latitude. A positive \(\beta_{L \cdot M}\) would indicate that the buffering provided by the leaf litter increases with latitude. We fit the model with the lme4 package and test fixed effects, and the latitudinal slope of each microhabitat (\(\beta_L\) for the canopy and \(\beta_L + \beta_{L \cdot M}\) for the leaf litter), with t-tests using the Kenward-Roger approximation for standard errors and degrees of freedom (pbkrtest package). The results of this model are presented in Table S7 of the manuscript.

  mutate(

buffer_data <- otms_data_sum |>
  mutate(
    lat_c = lat - mean_lat,
    microhabitat = factor(microhabitat, levels = c("branch", "leaflitter")),
    otm = paste(location, microhabitat, id),
    night = paste(location, date)
  )

# fit model
buffer_mod <- lme4::lmer(
  min_temp ~ lat_c * microhabitat + (1 | otm) + (1 | location) + (1 | night),
  data = buffer_data
)
# Kenward-Roger t-test and 95% CI for a linear combination of fixed effects
kr_test <- function(model, l) {
  vcov_kr <- as.matrix(pbkrtest::vcovAdj(model))
  est <- sum(l * lme4::fixef(model))
  se <- sqrt(as.numeric(t(l) %*% vcov_kr %*% l))
  ddf <- pbkrtest::get_Lb_ddf(model, l)
  t_value <- est / se
  tibble(
    estimate = est,
    se = se,
    df = ddf,
    t = t_value,
    p = 2 * pt(-abs(t_value), ddf),
    lower = est - qt(0.975, ddf) * se,
    upper = est + qt(0.975, ddf) * se
  )
}

# test each fixed effect
buffer_coefs <- purrr::map(
  seq_along(lme4::fixef(buffer_mod)),
  \(i) kr_test(buffer_mod, as.numeric(seq_along(lme4::fixef(buffer_mod)) == i))
) |>
  bind_rows() |>
  mutate(term = names(lme4::fixef(buffer_mod)))
